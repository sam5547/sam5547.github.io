RDE Structural Instrumentation

A low-cost structural instrumentation and data-analysis system for rotating detonation engine testing at UC's Gas Dynamics and Propulsion Laboratory.

The Problem

RDE structural measurements combine small strain signals with heat, vibration, electromagnetic interference, and high-frequency loading. The goal was a usable measurement chain at student-research scale, without relying on a conventional instrumentation package costing around $10,000.

Approach

I engineered a custom strain-measurement system for about $140 and ran modal FEA in SolidWorks to predict combustor natural frequencies and expected FFT peaks before testing. I then built a Python pipeline that converts 1 MHz strain-gauge data to microstrain, applies a Chebyshev Type II low-pass filter, and generates time-frequency spectrograms to compare structural response with chamber pressure.

Test failures drove repeated changes to shielding, grounding, amplifier placement, wiring, and high-temperature gauge bonding. The later aluminum enclosure came out of that iteration.

As a short side project, I also built an AD8421 instrumentation-amplifier circuit for the lab's thermocouples, giving ~166× gain (41 µV/°C to 6.8 mV/°C) for combustion diagnostics.

Multi-Nozzle Lunar Plume-Surface Interaction Test

A NASA-format research proposal for the first experimental comparison of single-, dual-, and quad-nozzle plume-surface interaction on lunar regolith simulant, written by a 7-person team from 7 universities through NASA's Proposal Writing and Evaluation Experience.

The Problem

When a lander fires its engines near the lunar surface, the exhaust blasts regolith outward at high speed, which can damage the lander and anything already on the ground. Future landers will use multiple engines, and simulations predict that interacting plumes can raise local ejecta mass flux by up to six times compared with a single engine. Every published PSI experiment so far has used a single nozzle, so those multi-nozzle predictions have never been checked against real data.

The Proposed Experiment

An atmospheric-pressure test rig runs four identical nozzles on regulated compressed air over a bed of BP-1 lunar simulant. The campaign steps from a single-nozzle baseline to dual- and quad-nozzle configurations, each at three spacings, across several heights and throttle settings. It measures ejecta velocity, spray angle, eroded mass, surface pressure, particle distribution, and crater shape using low-cost PIV, pressure sensors, high-speed video, and weighing, then compares the results against existing computational PSI models.

My Role

As mechanical design lead, I designed the modular test fixture: four aluminum nozzles on an adjustable aluminum-tube X-frame in a square layout, a 3D-printed PETG regolith tray, and an acrylic mounting plate. The frame lets nozzle count, spacing, and height change between runs without new hardware. It was designed to be fabricated in-house using a university machine shop and 3D printers.

I also contributed to the 12-month Phase B–F development plan, the $6,700 budget, the quad chart, and the New Technology Report. Earlier in the program, before the team settled on this concept, I was PI on one of five candidate concepts: an autonomous stabilization system for rotating detonation rocket engines.

Autonomous Multipurpose UAV

A modular quadcopter designed from scratch in Fusion 360 and built through rapid PETG prototyping, with a cylindrical fuselage and a removable avionics bay.

The Goal

Build a UAV airframe that stays easy to work on. Propulsion, batteries, flight electronics, and an onboard computer all have to fit in one fuselage without sacrificing structural integrity, cooling, component retention, or center of gravity.

Design

I own the mechanical architecture and structural design. The airframe uses a printed cylindrical fuselage with four bolt-on motor arms. The electronics mount to a separate sled that slides into the fuselage, so the whole avionics stack can come out for servicing instead of being rewired in place.

Every joint uses heat-set threaded inserts rather than screws driven straight into plastic, so parts can be taken apart and reassembled repeatedly during iteration without stripping.

Where It's At

The current revision is being redesigned around the removable, serviceable avionics bay, using lessons from packaging the electronics in the first build.
