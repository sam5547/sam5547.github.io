Multi-Nozzle Lunar Plume-Surface Interaction Test

A NASA-format research proposal for the first experimental comparison of single-, dual-, and quad-nozzle plume-surface interaction on lunar regolith simulant, written by a 7-person team from 7 universities through NASA's Proposal Writing and Evaluation Experience.

The Problem

When a lander fires its engines near the lunar surface, the exhaust blasts regolith outward at high speed, which can damage the lander and anything already on the ground. Future landers will use multiple engines, and simulations predict that interacting plumes can raise local ejecta mass flux by up to six times compared with a single engine. Every published PSI experiment so far has used a single nozzle, so those multi-nozzle predictions have never been checked against real data.

The Proposed Experiment

An atmospheric-pressure test rig runs four identical nozzles on regulated compressed air over a bed of BP-1 lunar simulant. The campaign steps from a single-nozzle baseline to dual- and quad-nozzle configurations, each at three spacings, across several heights and throttle settings. It measures ejecta velocity, spray angle, eroded mass, surface pressure, particle distribution, and crater shape using low-cost PIV, pressure sensors, high-speed video, and weighing, then compares the results against existing computational PSI models.

My Role

As mechanical design lead, I designed the modular test fixture: four aluminum nozzles on an adjustable aluminum-tube X-frame in a square layout, a 3D-printed PETG regolith tray, and an acrylic mounting plate. The frame lets nozzle count, spacing, and height change between runs without new hardware. It was designed to be fabricated in-house using a university machine shop and 3D printers.

I also contributed to the 12-month Phase B–F development plan, the $6,700 budget, the quad chart, and the New Technology Report. Earlier in the program, before the team settled on this concept, I was PI on one of five candidate concepts: an autonomous stabilization system for rotating detonation rocket engines.
